Seven Years, Well Spent: Why I Stepped Away, and What I Bring Back

AJ
Apoorva JoshiAI Solution Consultant (Presales) · Product Manager

There is a line on my CV that reads 2019 to 2026. On paper, it is just two dates and a silence.

I want to fill that silence, honestly.

In 2019 I stepped away from my career. Not because I ran out of ambition, but because the people I love needed more of me than a job would allow. I made that choice with open eyes. Looking back, it is one of the best decisions of my life.

This is the first post on this blog, and it felt wrong to begin anywhere else. If you have ever paused your own career for someone you love, whether a child, an ageing parent, a partner or simply yourself, this story is yours too.

Why I stepped away

I won't apologise for my reasons. I'll simply tell you them.

Home needed a steady centre. Ours is a small family of three, with no big household to absorb the strain when life gets heavy. Someone had to hold the centre. I chose to be that person, and I would choose it again.

My daughter needed me there, not just around. She was entering the years that shape everything: the exams, the big decisions, the quiet pressure of becoming who you are. I didn't want to give her what was left of me at the end of a long day. I wanted to give her the best of me. Today she studies at one of the UK's top universities. That success is entirely hers. But I was beside her for every late night along the way, and I wouldn't trade those nights for any promotion.

I was running on empty. Not tired the way a hard week leaves you, but worn down the way years of pushing do. So many of us know that feeling and keep going anyway. I chose to stop, heal properly, and find myself again.

What the gap actually held

People imagine a career break as an empty room. Mine was full of life, just without a job title.

  • School Parents' Association. I learned to lead people who owed me nothing. No reporting lines, no budget, very real deadlines. You deliver through goodwill and trust, or not at all.
  • School fairs. Stalls, volunteer rosters and a hundred moving parts, all landing on a single day. It is project management with balloons, and the deadline never moves.
  • The school family dinner. When the school needed a seating plan for around 900 guests, the request was simply to look at a spreadsheet. I treated it as the optimisation problem it really was, and seated everyone in an afternoon. Read how I did it →
  • Communal cooking. Cooking in large batches for school and community gatherings. Scaling a dish from a family dinner to hundreds of plates is planning, logistics and quality control, all served with love.
  • Education technology working group. Parents and staff, side by side, asking which tools truly helped children learn and which simply got in the way. I didn't call it product discovery then. I do now.
  • Tutoring. Nothing compares to the moment a stuck child suddenly gets it. It taught me more about clear communication than any boardroom ever did.
  • Art. It slowed me down. It taught me patience, and to look closely before I act.
  • AI. I watched it begin to change the world, and I refused to stay a spectator. I went from reading about it to building with it.

One thousand families, one front gate

The moment my break became something bigger came on an ordinary school event day.

Checking in more than 1,000 families had always been done by hand. It was a mammoth task. Volunteers dreaded it, families waited, and everyone accepted it because that was how it had always been done.

I couldn't accept it. So I built a simple HTML web page that let volunteers find and check in each family in moments. Simple solution - a siloed html page with strictly controlled distribution, that could be shift+deleted at the end of the day after downloading a report. No data security risk, no PDPA breached.

On the day, it just worked. Families walked straight in instead of queuing. Volunteers were free to welcome people instead of hunting through lists. I stood to one side and watched something I had built make a thousand small moments easier.

That was the day I realised I hadn't lost anything during my break. I had been gathering it.

Three builds, three different problems

That day lit a spark, and I followed it. Over the past year I have built three projects, each deliberately different from the last.

  • Attic December 2025
    An AI agent that clears a cluttered Gmail inbox. Letting an agent into someone's email is an act of trust, so I designed it to earn that trust before it earns any freedom.
  • BeatKeeper January 2026
    A team of AI agents that keeps busy experts visible, running a weekly content campaign from the news in their field. An expert's credibility is their business, so every claim is fact-checked, and nothing goes out without their own view and their approval.
  • MealPartner June 2026
    A WhatsApp assistant for university students facing the oldest question of student life: what shall I eat tonight? It uses retrieval-augmented generation, and I began with people, not code. Who is asking, what they really want, how the conversation should feel. It is designed for one user today and a hundred tomorrow.

Different domains, different technology, one habit I learned at home: start with the person on the other end. See the projects →

Why I am a strong hire, now

I am not returning despite these seven years. I am returning because of them.

  • I lead without a title. Volunteer work means moving people with no authority and no budget. That is exactly what presales and product roles ask for every day.
  • I listen before I build. The check-in page and MealPartner both started with understanding people, not writing code.
  • I build with AI, hands-on. I have designed agents and retrieval systems myself, not just read about them.
  • I translate between worlds. Parents, teachers, volunteers and engineers speak different languages. Bridging them is the daily work of an AI solution consultant or a product manager.
  • I come back whole. Rested, focused and certain. I chose this return as deliberately as I chose the break.

I am pursuing roles in AI solution consulting and product management. I bring a builder's instinct, genuine empathy, and the steadiness of someone who knows exactly why she is here.

Coming back, on purpose

The day my daughter left for university, the house went very quiet. I felt two things at once: an ache for the years that had flown, and a clear, bright readiness for what comes next.

She has started writing her own chapter. It is time I picked up my pen again.

To every mother and father who stepped back, every son or daughter who paused to care for a parent, every person who chose people over a promotion for a while: that time was not lost. It shaped you. Say so, without apology.

This blog is where I will write in public. Years ago I drafted a post on optimisation versus machine learning and never finished it. It is finally published. The drafts waited for me. So did the work. I am ready for both.
