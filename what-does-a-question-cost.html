What Does a Question Cost? Pricing an AI Review, One Model Call at a Time

AJ
Apoorva JoshiAI Solution Consultant (Presales) · Product Manager

After I wrote about building Quaestio, the question I heard most wasn't about privacy or academic integrity. It was a budget question: what does one review actually cost, and who pays for it?

I recognised it straight away. In my presales years, that was the question that quietly decided deals. A buyer can love your demo, but if you can't say what the thing costs to run, and why, the conversation stalls.

It matters even more for Quaestio. In Building Quaestio I explained that it's licensed per student, and each student gets an allowance of reviews per subject, two by default. If the cost of a review swings wildly from one draft to the next, that allowance becomes hard to price honestly. So before promising any price to a school, I built a small simulation of the review pipeline and ran six drafts through it, priced call by call.

Three models, three jobs

A review isn't one call to one AI model. It's a short chain, and each link uses the cheapest model that does that job well. Only the feedback itself goes to the frontier model. This was one of the decisions I made in the requirements; the receipts are the first time I've seen what it's worth.

Checks the draft is a genuine, complete-enough piece of coursework before a review is used. Then checks every question Opus writes, to confirm it contains no rewrites or text a student could paste in.Readiness gate · guardrail check

What the receipts show

2.1×cost of the weakest draft over the strongest

Draft quality drives the cost, not draft length

The poor maths draft has the fewest words of the six, yet it costs more than twice as much to review as the excellent biology draft. A weak draft takes the model longer to reason through, earns more questions, and prompts more follow-ups. The student's own text is a small slice of the bill. The model's thinking and writing are most of it.

That surprised me at first. My instinct, like most people's, was to price by the page. But the receipts say the work is in the reading and reasoning, not in the word count.

A second draft costs the same as a first, for the same quality

Every review is independent by design. Quaestio never sends a previous draft or earlier feedback with a request, so nothing about a student's work is carried between sessions. The physics draft that stayed average costs the same both times, within about 1%. The maths draft gets cheaper only because the student improved it.

I could have saved a little by giving the model the earlier feedback as context. I chose not to. "Never remember" was one of my five principles, and it turns out to have a price tag, a small one, that I'm happy to pay.

The frontier model is most of the bill, and that's where it should be

Opus accounts for roughly four-fifths of the cost of every review. Running the readiness gate, the guardrail check and the follow-ups on smaller models saves about 31% compared with sending everything to Opus. Try the toggle below to see the difference.

What this means for a school

With the default allowance of two reviews per subject, the model cost for one student in one subject lands between roughly S$0.40 and S$0.64 in these scenarios. A student taking all four pilot subjects, with weak first drafts in every one, would stay under about S$2.60 for the year.

That figure is small, and predictable enough to build into a per-student licence. The larger costs of running Quaestio sit elsewhere: secure hosting in Singapore, redaction, the subject-teacher time that goes into the knowledge base, and support for schools. Model cost is the part that scales with use, which is why it's worth knowing precisely.

It also answers a quieter worry. Because a weaker draft costs more to review, the students who need the most help are the ones who use the most of the model. The allowance is the same for everyone. The effort the model spends isn't, and it shouldn't be.

Know what each unit of value costs you before you promise anyone a price.

How the numbers were built

I'd rather say this plainly: these are simulated reviews, not measurements from live use. The token counts below are my working assumptions, and I'll replace them with real figures from the pilot. Prices are Anthropic's published API rates for October 2026.

DraftWordsDraft tokensOpus thinkingQuestionsFollow-upsTotal (SGD)
  • Prices per million tokens (USD): Opus 5.5 $4 in / $20 out; Sonnet 5.5 $2 / $10; Haiku 5.5 $0.10 / $0.50. Cached reads are $0.20, $0.10 and $0.01 respectively. Converted at US$1 = S$1.28.
  • Draft size: about 1.7 tokens per word after redaction; maths drafts run denser because equations travel as LaTeX.
  • Shared material is cached: instructions, criterion checklists and knowledge-base entries (about 18,000 tokens for the main review) are billed at the cached rate. Student text is never cached.
  • Each question is about 90 tokens of structured output: the question, the criterion it relates to, and where it points in the draft.
  • Each follow-up sends about 1,200 tokens plus 3,000 cached, and returns about 700.
  • Not included: the occasional cache write when a cache expires, retries, and the premium for US-only data residency, which a Singapore launch doesn't need.

Four things I learned

  1. Price the work, not the word count. The obvious unit, pages or words, was the wrong one. What drives cost is how hard the draft is to reason about. Find the real cost driver before you pick a pricing unit, or your margins will depend on luck.
  2. Model routing is a commercial decision too. I chose to route tasks to smaller models for speed and safety. The receipts showed it's also worth almost a third of the bill. Architecture choices and pricing choices are the same conversation.
  3. Principles have a price, so put a number on it. Never carrying a student's work between sessions costs a little more per review. Knowing exactly how much made it easy to defend, instead of a vague worry that privacy is expensive.
  4. Publish your assumptions with your numbers. A cost model without its assumptions is just a claim. Showing the token counts, the rates and what's left out invites a school's finance team to check my working, and that builds more trust than a single tidy figure.

As with the specification, I built this simulation working with Claude. I set the scenarios and the assumptions and decided what the numbers meant; Claude helped turn them into a working model and the receipts above, and kept me honest about what a simulation can and can't claim.

What's next

The next step is to replace every assumption in that table with a measurement. During the pilot I'll track real token counts per review, how many follow-ups students actually use, and whether weaker drafts really do cost more in practice. Then the licence price can rest on evidence, not a spreadsheet of guesses.
